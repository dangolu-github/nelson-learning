1. Start with your ideas

Speak first. Then capture only your most useful ideas.

Which helps you learn science better: reading an explanation, watching a demonstration, or doing an experiment yourself? Why?

2. The reading decision

For every statement, compare its meaning with the passage. Do not use outside knowledge.

TRUEThe passage says the same thing.
FALSEThe passage says the opposite.
NOT GIVENThe passage does not answer the statement.

Worked example

Text: The library closes at 6 p.m.

A. The library is open after 7 p.m. → FALSE because closes at 6 gives the opposite meaning.

B. The library is busiest at 5 p.m. → NOT GIVEN because the text gives no information about how busy it is.

3. Reading: a life in science

Read normally. Then answer one statement at a time and underline the shortest evidence that proves your choice.

Marie Curie: research, discovery and teaching

The marriage of Pierre and Marie Curie in 1895 began a scientific partnership that soon produced important results. After Henri Becquerel discovered a new phenomenon in 1896, Marie investigated whether radioactivity was present in elements other than uranium. She found that thorium had the same property.

Marie then studied pitchblende, a mineral that was more radioactive than pure uranium. This suggested that the mineral contained a small amount of an unknown, highly active substance. Pierre joined her research, and their work led to the discovery of two new elements: polonium and radium. Pierre mainly studied the new forms of radiation, while Marie worked to obtain pure radium. In 1903, Marie and Pierre shared the Nobel Prize for Physics with Becquerel.

The births of Marie’s daughters did not interrupt her scientific work. She also taught physics and introduced lessons based on experimental demonstrations. After Pierre died in 1906, Marie continued their work alone and became the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne. In 1911, she received the Nobel Prize for Chemistry for isolating pure radium.

5. From a spoken idea to a developed paragraph

Writing focus: Schools should use more practical experiments instead of relying mainly on textbooks. Do you agree or disagree?
POINTGive your clear answer or main idea.
WHYExplain why the idea is reasonable.
EXAMPLEMake the explanation concrete.
RESULTShow the effect and link back.

Model on a different topic

Students should study in groups more often. I agree because group work allows learners to explain difficult ideas to one another. For example, when students solve a maths problem together, one person may show a method that the others have not considered. As a result, everyone can understand the problem more deeply and become more confident. Therefore, regular group study can support learning when each student participates actively.

Notice 1
Underline the writer’s clear position.
Notice 2
Number the POINT, WHY, EXAMPLE and RESULT.
Notice 3
Mark where the writer moves from reason to evidence and from evidence to result.

Idea bank: choose one route

Do not use every idea. Select one route and turn its connected statements into one developed paragraph.

Route A: deeper understanding

  • Scientific explanations in textbooks can be abstract.
  • Experiments allow students to observe a principle in action.
  • Learners can compare a prediction with an actual result.
  • This connects theory with evidence and improves retention.
  • Example: compare how efficiently different materials conduct heat.

Route B: scientific thinking

  • Science involves testing claims, not merely memorising facts.
  • Students formulate a hypothesis and control the variables.
  • They record, compare and interpret the results.
  • An unexpected result encourages them to revise their explanation.
  • This develops practical and analytical skills.

Route C: a balanced approach

  • Practical lessons require equipment, time and safe supervision.
  • Some experiments are too costly or hazardous for individual students.
  • Textbooks efficiently provide background knowledge and instructions.
  • Teachers can use demonstrations or simulations when necessary.
  • Schools should supplement theory with regular practical work, not abandon books.
