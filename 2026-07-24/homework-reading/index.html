Text 1 — Digital Habits Across Generations

Reading passage

Digital habits across generations

People of different ages often use digital technology in very different ways. In the UK, more adults over 55 are joining Facebook. There are about 3.5 million users aged 55–64, compared with 2.9 million users over 65.

Sheila is 59. She opened a Facebook account because her daughter shares photos and videos of Sheila’s grandchildren there. When Sheila was young, families had to wait for letters and printed photographs. Now she can follow much more of her grandchildren’s daily lives.

Unlike Sheila, many teenagers are becoming less interested in Facebook. However, this does not mean they are leaving their smartphones behind. Chloe, who is 15, keeps her phone beside her even while she sleeps. She uses it as an alarm clock, looks at it before going to sleep and checks it again as soon as she wakes up.

Heavy phone use can affect friendships. Some teenagers spend so much time online at home that they miss out on meeting friends face to face. Sheila has had the opposite experience: social media helped her contact school friends she had not heard from for forty years. They now use Facebook to arrange meetings in different parts of the country.

Parents may have helped create today’s smartphone addiction because many of them were early adopters. Peter, a 38-year-old father of two teenagers, once used his phone or laptop constantly. He felt that he was always connected to his work. He then realised that he could not ask his children to reduce their screen time while he was always looking at a screen himself.

In the evenings and at weekends, Peter now moves his SIM card into a simple mobile phone. It can make calls and send text messages, but it has no smartphone apps. He is not completely cut off from other people in an emergency, and he can set a better example while spending more quality time with his family.

Text 2 — How to Spot Fake News

Reading passage

How to spot fake news

When you go online, pictures, videos, links and articles can bombard you with information. Some stories are false. Their creators may want to earn money from advertising or influence people’s political opinions. False stories can spread very quickly because sharing them takes only a few seconds.

Some fake news is obviously unbelievable, but other misinformation is subtle and much harder to notice. Researchers who study media and online behaviour are examining this phenomenon. The four checks below can help you avoid being fooled.

Section 1 — Check the source

First, examine the website that published the story. Ask whether it looks professional, whether the writing is clear and whether it contains a range of news reports. A doubtful website may copy the name or address of a real newspaper but publish very little reliable information. Its About page should clearly explain the organisation behind the site.

Section 2 — Examine the pictures

A photograph may have been edited or taken from an unrelated event. Look carefully for signs that the image was changed. You can also use Google Reverse Image Search to find other places where the same picture appeared and discover whether it was used in a different context.

Section 3 — Compare other reports

Search for the same story on news websites that you already know and trust. If several large and reliable organisations report the same event, the story is less likely to be fake, although exceptions are possible. Established news organisations usually check their sources before publishing.

Section 4 — Notice style and emotion

Fake news pages may use ALL CAPS, dramatic language and many pop-up adverts. Pay attention to your emotional reaction too. A story that immediately makes you extremely angry may have been designed to create that feeling. When you know that a story is false, do not share it.
