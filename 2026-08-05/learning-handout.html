Grammar and academic vocabulary

Irregular Verbs, Fiction, and Genre

Review three-part verb forms, then use clear definitions and examples for literature and film vocabulary.

1. Learn the complete verb set

An irregular verb does not make its past forms by simply adding -ed. Say and remember the base form, past form and past participle together.

begin · began · begun

We began the quiz in class. We have begun the review.

buy · bought · bought

She bought a book. She has bought the correct edition.

Notice spelling, sound and vowel changes. Retrieval is stronger when you say the three forms before using one in a sentence.

2. Meaning depends on context

beat

As a verb, it can mean to hit something repeatedly. It can also mean to defeat someone in a game or competition.

burst and explode

A balloon can burst or pop. Explode usually suggests a more violent release of energy, often involving a larger object or event.

3. Fiction and non-fiction

Fiction

Writing created from imagination, such as a novel or short story. Fiction can use real places or events, but the story itself is invented.

Non-fiction

Writing that presents factual information about real people, events or ideas, such as a biography, news report or history book.

Quick distinction: fiction tells an imagined story; non-fiction aims to inform us about the real world.

4. Genre and adaptation

Genre means a type or category of literature or film. Examples include drama, action, comedy and history. Fiction/non-fiction tells us whether the main content is imagined or factual; genre tells us what kind of work it is.

Adaptation: a story changed into another form.

A novel made into a film is a film adaptation of the novel.

5. Wider academic discussion

We briefly discussed university applications, academic competitions and how personal strengths and interests can help a student identify suitable opportunities. Requirements vary between institutions, so any future application decision must be checked against the exact programme.
